THE WORKOUT LIBRARY

Know what you're training.
Know why it works.

A practical reference to cycling sessions, strength training, skills and recovery. Use it to understand your options and build your own training—not to collect hard sessions for the sake of it.

Before you start

These are example sessions, not a personalised prescription. Choose sessions that match your experience, health, available time and current training. Build gradually, keep most training easy, and avoid stacking hard days. Stop for sharp pain, dizziness, chest pain or unusual symptoms; seek qualified advice when needed. If you are new to structured training or lifting, learn technique with a qualified coach before adding load.

ON THE BIKE

Cycling sessions

Each session has a different training effect. The right choice depends on the goal and the rider's current training base.

POWER · ACCELERATIONAdvanced intensity

Short sprints

Develop rapid force production, acceleration and the ability to respond to surges—useful for XC starts, steep ramps and attacks.

Example session

  • Warm up for 15–20 minutes, including 3 short progressive efforts.
  • 6–8 × 8–12 seconds near-maximal sprint from a rolling start or safe low-speed start.
  • Recover for 3–5 minutes of very easy riding between efforts.
  • Cool down for 10 minutes.

Execution: Prioritise a powerful, controlled acceleration and smooth pedal stroke. Use a safe, straight, traffic-free surface or a suitable trainer. Stop the set when power, speed or technique clearly drops.

Why the recovery is long: These efforts train high-quality peak power. Short recoveries turn them into a different, more fatiguing workout.

Common mistake: Doing lots of all-out sprints while already tired. Quality matters more than the number of repetitions.

Typical load: low total work, high peak effortBest placed when fresh
POWER · STARTSIntermediate–advanced

Hill accelerations

Practise accelerating against resistance, helping develop start power and the ability to get up to speed on a climb.

Example session

  • Warm up for 15–20 minutes.
  • 4–6 × 10–15 seconds accelerating up a moderate, safe gradient.
  • Recover fully for 3–4 minutes by riding easily back down or on the flat.
  • Cool down for 10 minutes.

Execution: Start at a controlled speed, then accelerate smoothly. Keep the bike stable and choose a gear that lets you accelerate without grinding at an extremely low cadence.

Why it helps: The gradient increases resistance and makes you apply force while maintaining control. It is specific to climbing acceleration, but does not replace general strength or aerobic work.

Common mistake: Turning every repetition into a slow, maximal grind. That can overload knees and compromise technique.

Typical load: low volume, high intensityAllow full recovery
AEROBIC POWERAdvanced intensity

VO₂ max intervals

Challenge the body's ability to take in and use oxygen at high exercise intensities. Useful for repeated hard climbs and sustained race surges.

Example session

  • Warm up for 15–20 minutes, gradually increasing effort.
  • Start with 4 × 3 minutes hard but controlled, around 8–9/10 effort.
  • Ride easily for 3 minutes between repetitions.
  • Cool down for 10–15 minutes.

Execution: Aim for consistent efforts. The first repetition should not be so hard that the final one collapses. On a power meter, use an individually tested target; without one, use breathing and perceived effort.

Progression: Add a repetition or extend intervals gradually, not both at once. A common later format is 4–5 × 4 minutes, but it is not a starting point for everyone.

Common mistake: Sprinting the opening minute. VO₂ work is hard, sustained aerobic work—not a series of all-out sprints.

Typical load: highUsually 1 session per week or less
THRESHOLDIntermediate–advanced

Threshold intervals

Improve the ability to sustain a strong, steady effort around the upper limit of predominantly aerobic work. Useful for long climbs and hard race sections.

Example session

  • Warm up for 15 minutes.
  • 3 × 8 minutes at a hard, sustainable effort (about 7–8/10).
  • Recover for 4 minutes easy between intervals.
  • Cool down for 10 minutes.

Execution: Keep effort even. You should be working hard but not sprinting or gasping uncontrollably. Power targets require a recent, reliable test and should be adjusted as fitness changes.

Progression: Gradually build total time near threshold, for example from 3 × 8 to 3 × 10 minutes, with recovery and total load considered.

Common mistake: Treating threshold as an all-out time trial. Going too hard changes the session and makes completion inconsistent.

Typical load: highNeeds recovery afterwards
SUSTAINED AEROBICIntermediate

Tempo and sweet spot

Build comfort and durability at a steady, moderately hard intensity. Can be time-efficient when training time is limited, but is more fatiguing than easy endurance.

Example session

  • Warm up for 15 minutes.
  • 2 × 15 minutes at controlled tempo to upper-tempo effort (about 6–7/10).
  • Recover for 5 minutes easy.
  • Cool down for 10 minutes.

Execution: Maintain a smooth cadence and an effort you can sustain without straining. Sweet spot is a power-based zone below threshold; do not guess exact wattage without a suitable FTP estimate.

When to use it: As a bridge between easy endurance and harder threshold work, or when a longer ride needs some structure.

Common mistake: Making every endurance ride tempo. That removes easy volume and can leave too little recovery for genuinely hard sessions.

Typical load: moderate–highNot an easy day
AEROBIC BASEAll levels

Endurance ride

Develop aerobic capacity and the ability to ride for longer while supporting recovery between harder sessions. This is the foundation of most XC training.

Example session

  • Ride for 60–120 minutes at an easy, steady effort.
  • Keep breathing controlled; you should generally be able to speak in full sentences.
  • Choose terrain that allows the effort to remain mostly steady.

Execution: Avoid chasing speed on climbs. Eat and drink appropriately for the duration and conditions. Beginners should start shorter and extend gradually.

Why it matters: Easy volume develops aerobic adaptations with a lower recovery cost than repeated hard sessions. It also builds time in the saddle and pacing discipline.

Common mistake: Riding too hard because the pace feels easy. If every ride becomes a moderate grind, fatigue accumulates.

Typical load: low–moderateCan be repeated regularly
RACE SPECIFICAdvanced

Over-under intervals

Practise changing intensity around threshold, similar to rolling terrain where climbs, corners and accelerations interrupt a hard steady effort.

Example session

  • Warm up for 15–20 minutes.
  • 3 × 8 minutes alternating 2 minutes controlled just below threshold and 1 minute just above threshold, repeated within each block.
  • Recover for 5 minutes easy between blocks.
  • Cool down for 10 minutes.

Execution: The harder minutes should be firm, not maximal. Settle quickly after each surge and keep the whole block controlled.

Why it helps XC: Cross-country racing rarely holds one perfectly steady intensity. This session practises recovering while continuing to work.

Common mistake: Making the over sections sprints. That turns a controlled aerobic session into a much more costly workout.

Typical load: highUse after a solid base
REPEATED SURGESAdvanced

Microbursts

Train repeated short hard surges with incomplete recovery. Relevant to repeated accelerations out of corners and over short rises.

Example session

  • Warm up for 15–20 minutes.
  • 2 sets of 6–8 minutes alternating 30 seconds hard and 30 seconds easy.
  • Recover for 5–6 minutes between sets.
  • Cool down for 10 minutes.

Execution: Hard means strong and repeatable, not a maximal sprint. Keep the easy periods genuinely easy so you can maintain the set.

Progression: Begin with one set or fewer repetitions. Add volume only when you can complete all efforts consistently.

Common mistake: Starting too hard and fading sharply. The training effect depends on repeatability.

Typical load: highRequires good recovery
PEDALLING SKILLAll levels

Cadence drills

Improve comfort and coordination across different pedalling rates. Useful for adapting to changing gradients and gears.

Example session

  • During an easy ride, complete 4–6 × 1 minute at a faster, smooth cadence.
  • Ride easily for 2 minutes between drills.
  • Stay seated and relaxed; keep the upper body quiet.

Execution: Increase cadence gradually without bouncing in the saddle. These should not be maximal efforts.

Why it helps: Cadence practice builds coordination and awareness. It is not a magic shortcut to higher fitness or power.

Common mistake: Spinning so fast that the hips bounce or the pedal stroke becomes uncontrolled.

Typical load: lowFits inside an easy ride

OFF THE BIKE

Gym workouts

Strength training can improve force production, robustness and movement capacity. For cycling, the goal is to complement riding—not make every gym day exhausting.

LOWER BODY · STRENGTHAll levels with coaching

Lower-body strength

Build general leg strength to support force production on climbs, starts and repeated hard pedalling.

Example workout

  • Squat variation: 3 × 5–8 reps.
  • Romanian deadlift: 3 × 6–10 reps.
  • Split squat: 2–3 × 8 reps per leg.
  • Calf raise: 2 × 10–15 reps.

Execution: Choose a load that allows controlled repetitions and leaves roughly 2–3 good reps in reserve. Use a comfortable range of motion. Rest 2–3 minutes on the main lifts and 1–2 minutes on accessories.

Why these movements: Squats train coordinated leg force; hip hinges train the posterior chain; split squats challenge each leg independently; calf raises train the lower leg.

Common mistake: Adding weight before technique is consistent. Stop if a movement causes pain rather than normal muscular effort.

Typical load: moderate–highAllow 48 hours before hard leg work
UNILATERAL STRENGTHIntermediate

Single-leg strength

Develop strength and control on each side independently, with balance and pelvic stability demands relevant to riding position and off-bike movement.

Example workout

  • Step-ups: 3 × 6–8 per leg.
  • Reverse lunges: 3 × 8 per leg.
  • Single-leg Romanian deadlift: 2 × 8 per leg.
  • Side plank: 2 × 20–40 seconds per side.

Execution: Use a step height that allows the whole foot to stay planted and the knee to track comfortably. Move slowly enough to control the lowering phase.

Why it helps: It exposes side-to-side differences and trains control under load. It does not guarantee equal power on the bike.

Common mistake: Using a box that is too high, pushing excessively from the trailing foot or allowing the knee to collapse inward.

Typical load: moderatePrioritise control over load
POWER · EXPLOSIVEAdvanced / coached

Explosive power

Train the ability to produce force quickly. This can complement sprint and acceleration work on the bike.

Example session

  • Box jumps or squat jumps: 3 × 3–5.
  • Medicine-ball chest pass or slam: 3 × 4–6.
  • Light kettlebell swing, if technique is established: 3 × 6–8.

Execution: Use low repetition counts, full rest (2–3 minutes) and stop when jump height, speed or landing quality falls. Land quietly with knees and hips controlled.

Why it helps: Explosive movements train rapid force expression. Transfer to cycling is plausible but depends on the exercise, athlete and training programme; it is not automatic.

Common mistake: Doing jumps when exhausted or chasing fatigue. Power work should be crisp, not a conditioning circuit.

Typical load: low volume, high intentDo while fresh
UPPER BODYAll levels

Upper-body strength

Build general strength and support posture, bike handling and the ability to tolerate longer time in an aggressive riding position.

Example workout

  • Push-up or bench press: 3 × 6–10 reps.
  • Row variation: 3 × 8–12 reps.
  • Lat pulldown or assisted pull-up: 3 × 8–10 reps.
  • Landmine press or light overhead press: 2 × 8–10 reps.

Execution: Balance pushing with pulling. Keep shoulder blades controlled and avoid painful ranges. Use a stable bench and appropriate supervision for free-weight pressing.

Why it helps: Upper-body strength supports general athleticism and bike control, especially on rough terrain. It is secondary to leg strength and cycling-specific work for pedalling performance.

Common mistake: Training only chest and arms while neglecting back, shoulder control and trunk stability.

Typical load: moderate1–2 sessions weekly if tolerated
TRUNK · STABILITYAll levels

Core and trunk stability

Train the trunk to resist unwanted movement and transfer force between the upper and lower body while riding.

Example workout

  • Dead bug: 3 × 6–10 per side.
  • Side plank: 2–3 × 20–40 seconds per side.
  • Pallof press: 3 × 8–12 per side.
  • Bird dog: 2 × 8 per side.

Execution: Move slowly, breathe normally and keep the pelvis and ribs controlled. Increase difficulty only when you can maintain position.

Why it helps: Trunk endurance and control may help maintain riding posture and handle repeated bike movement. Endless sit-ups are not required.

Common mistake: Holding your breath or allowing the lower back to arch to complete a repetition.

Typical load: low–moderateCan pair with upper-body work
POSTERIOR CHAINIntermediate

Glutes and hamstrings

Strengthen hip extension and the muscles at the back of the thigh, which contribute to general lower-body force production and movement control.

Example workout

  • Hip thrust or glute bridge: 3 × 8–12.
  • Romanian deadlift: 3 × 6–10.
  • Hamstring curl: 2–3 × 10–12.
  • Bird dog: 2 × 8 per side.

Execution: Keep the hinge controlled and the spine stable. A Romanian deadlift should come from hip movement, not from reaching the weights towards the floor at all costs.

Why it helps: It develops the posterior chain as part of balanced leg strength. No single muscle group works in isolation during cycling.

Common mistake: Forcing extra range when hamstring mobility or control is limiting. Reduce range or choose another hinge variation.

Typical load: moderateProgress gradually
GENERAL CONDITIONINGBeginner–intermediate

Full-body strength circuit

Practise basic movement patterns and build general muscular endurance when equipment or time is limited.

Example circuit

  • Goblet squat: 8–10 reps.
  • Incline push-up: 8–12 reps.
  • Resistance-band row: 10–12 reps.
  • Glute bridge: 12 reps.
  • Dead bug: 8 per side.
  • Complete 2–3 rounds, resting as needed.

Execution: Keep every repetition controlled. Finish each round feeling that you could still perform a few good repetitions. This is not a race.

Why it helps: A circuit can make consistent general training accessible. It is less suitable for maximising strength or power than focused sets with longer rest.

Common mistake: Turning it into a breathless competition and letting form deteriorate.

Typical load: moderateUseful entry point

RIDE BETTER

Skills and technique

Fitness only helps if you can use it on the course. Skills sessions should be deliberate, progressive and performed in a safe environment.

BIKE HANDLINGAll levels

Cornering and braking drills

Improve speed control, line choice and confidence through corners—often a major source of time on an XC course.

Example session · 20–30 minutes

  • Choose a flat, open, traffic-free practice area.
  • Practise braking in a straight line before the turn.
  • Ride repeated wide turns, gradually tightening the line while staying controlled.
  • Focus on looking through the exit, balanced body position and smooth release of brakes.

Progression: Practise one variable at a time, then transfer the skill to easy trail sections before increasing speed.

Common mistake: Braking hard while leaned over, looking at the front wheel or trying to copy race speed before control is reliable.

Typical load: low physical loadHigh concentration
CLIMBING SKILLAll levels

Climbing technique

Improve traction, pacing, gear choice and body position on technical or steep climbs.

Example session · 20–40 minutes

  • Choose a climb with a safe run-out and manageable gradient.
  • Complete 4–6 controlled ascents, not maximal efforts.
  • Practise seated traction, smooth pedal pressure and looking ahead.
  • On suitable terrain, compare seated and standing approaches.

Execution: Keep weight balanced so the rear wheel maintains grip while the front wheel remains controlled. Shift before the steepest section where possible.

Common mistake: Attacking every climb and spinning the rear wheel. The aim is clean, repeatable technique.

Typical load: low–moderateCan combine with endurance riding
DESCENDINGProgressive

Descending and line choice

Build control, vision and confidence on descents. Better technique can save energy and reduce mistakes during a race.

Example session · 20–30 minutes

  • Start on a familiar, easy trail feature.
  • Walk unfamiliar or consequential features first.
  • Practise looking ahead, neutral position and controlled braking.
  • Repeat at a speed where the line remains deliberate and safe.

Progression: Increase difficulty only after consistency on the easier feature. A qualified skills coach can identify habits that are hard to spot alone.

Common mistake: Treating fear as something to override. Build skill and confidence progressively; use appropriate protective equipment.

Typical load: variablePrioritise safety

ADAPTATION HAPPENS BETWEEN SESSIONS

Recovery sessions

Recovery is not wasted training time. It allows the body to adapt to the work already completed.

ACTIVE RECOVERYEasy

Recovery spin

Add gentle movement without creating much additional training stress. It can help a rider feel less stiff, but it is not essential.

Example session

  • 15–30 minutes very easy cycling.
  • Use light resistance and a comfortable cadence.
  • Finish feeling the same or better than when you started.

Execution: Keep effort around 1–2/10. If you feel worse, stop and rest instead.

Common mistake: Letting an easy spin become another workout because the legs begin to feel good.

Typical load: very lowOptional
MOBILITYAll levels

Mobility routine

Maintain comfortable movement and prepare for positions used in training. Mobility work should address an individual's limitations rather than chase extreme flexibility.

Example routine · 8–12 minutes

  • Cat-cow: 6–8 controlled repetitions.
  • Hip flexor dynamic stretch: 6 per side.
  • Ankle rocks: 8 per side.
  • Thoracic rotations: 6 per side.
  • Bodyweight squat to comfortable depth: 8 reps.

Execution: Move smoothly and stay within a comfortable range. Use dynamic movements before riding; gentle static stretches can be used after training if they feel useful.

Common mistake: Forcing a stretch through pain or assuming stretching alone prevents injury.

Typical load: very lowCan be done regularly
REST AND READINESSEssential

Rest day

Allow recovery from accumulated physical and mental stress. A rest day can be more productive than forcing a session when run down.

What to prioritise

  • Get sufficient sleep on a consistent schedule.
  • Eat enough to support growth, daily life and training.
  • Drink to thirst and replace fluids after substantial sweating.
  • Keep optional activity genuinely gentle.

When to rest: Persistent fatigue, worsening performance, illness, unusual soreness or pain are reasons to reduce training and consider professional advice.

Common mistake: Feeling guilty about rest and replacing missed sessions with extra hard work.

Training load: none plannedPart of the programme

USE THE LIBRARY WELL

Training principles

1. Specificity

Training should reflect the demands of the goal. XC riders need aerobic endurance, the ability to surge repeatedly, climbing strength, handling skill and race practice. No single workout develops all of these.

2. Progressive overload

Adaptation requires a gradually increasing challenge, but increases should be small enough to recover from. Change one variable at a time—such as repetitions, duration or load—and monitor how you respond.

3. Consistency beats hero sessions

A repeatable week of mostly manageable training is more useful than occasional extreme efforts followed by exhaustion. Hard sessions should have a clear purpose.

4. Easy means easy

Easy endurance and recovery sessions create room for quality work. If every ride is moderately hard, fatigue can accumulate and the hardest sessions lose quality.

5. Recovery is part of training

Sleep, adequate food, hydration and rest affect adaptation. Do not use a load score as permission to ignore pain, illness or persistent fatigue.

6. Individual response varies

Training zones, recovery needs and tolerable volume differ. Power zones need reliable testing; RPE is useful but subjective. Adjust sessions to the athlete, not the other way around.

How to use these sessions in a week

For most developing riders, start with one demanding cycling session, one or two manageable strength sessions and easy endurance or skills work around them. Keep hard sessions separated by enough recovery, and account for football, swimming, school, work and other sport as real training load. This is a broad starting principle, not a fixed weekly prescription.

The library describes established training concepts in general terms. It is educational material, not medical advice or a substitute for an individual plan from a qualified coach. Exercise selection and loading should be adapted to experience, equipment and supervision.
